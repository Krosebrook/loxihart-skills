# Five-Second Tests and Concept Testing

## Five-Second Tests

Five-second tests measure first impressions and immediate comprehension. The design is shown for exactly 5 seconds, then removed.

**What they reveal:**
- What users notice first (attention hierarchy)
- Whether the primary value proposition lands immediately
- What users remember after a brief exposure
- Whether brand tone is communicated correctly

**What they don't reveal:** usability, task flow, detailed comprehension, or any behavior requiring reading time.

### Process

1. Recruit 5–10 participants (unmoderated works well for this)
2. Show the design for exactly 5 seconds
3. Remove the design entirely before asking questions
4. Ask (pick 2–3, not all):
   - "What do you remember seeing?"
   - "What is this product/page for?"
   - "Who do you think this is for?"
   - "What was the most prominent element?"
5. Analyze: correct comprehension rate, most common first impressions, what dominated attention

### Use For
- Landing pages: is the value proposition immediately clear?
- Dashboard first views: is the most important information visible without hunting?
- New feature introductions: does the purpose land in 5 seconds?
- Brand refresh validation: does the new direction communicate the right tone?

### Do Not Use For
Complex flows, multi-step tasks, content-heavy pages, or anything requiring reading time to evaluate. A user can't evaluate a 10-step wizard in 5 seconds.

**Tools:** Maze, Lyssna (formerly UsabilityHub)

## Concept Testing

Concept testing evaluates an idea before it's fully designed. Goal: is this the right direction, not is this design usable.

### Desirability Testing (Microsoft Product Reaction Cards)

Present a design, description, or prototype. Ask participants to choose 5 words from the 118 Microsoft Product Reaction Cards that best describe their experience or reaction.

Tally responses across participants — themes emerge quickly. Words like "overwhelming", "confusing", "professional", "innovative" reveal emotional response and brand alignment before a single pixel of final UI is built.

Works for: evaluating design direction, brand tone, and emotional response. Does not reveal task flow issues.

### Assumption Mapping

List the core assumptions behind the concept. Rate each on two axes:
- **Certainty** — how confident are you this assumption is true? (1–5)
- **Importance** — how much does the concept depend on this being true? (1–5)

Test high-importance, low-certainty assumptions first. These are your riskiest bets. Don't build the full product to discover they're wrong.

### Concept Comparison

Show 2–3 distinct concept directions (different mental models, not just different visual treatments). Ask:
- Which resonates most? Why?
- What's missing from each?
- What would make you choose this over your current approach?

**Never ask which is "better."** Users reliably bias toward the most concrete or detailed option, not the best concept.

### Prototype Concept Test

Clickable or paper prototype. 3–5 participants. Moderated.

Goal: does the concept make sense? Can users articulate what they'd do here? What surprises them?

This is different from a usability test — you're not measuring task success rates. You're validating mental model fit. Use think-aloud protocol and probe on moments of confusion.
